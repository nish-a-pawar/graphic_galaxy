# Packaging Design: What Should a Good Product Package Actually Do?

A product may be excellent, but the customer doesn't experience that quality until they try it.

Before that happens, the packaging is often doing the first job: getting the product noticed and helping the customer understand what it is.

That's why packaging design is more than making a pouch, box or bottle look attractive.

## Good packaging has a job to do

Walk into a shop and look at a shelf full of similar products.

You usually don't study every package.

You notice a few things quickly:
- What is the product?
- Which brand is it?
- What makes it different?
- Does it look trustworthy?
- Is the information easy to understand?

Good packaging helps answer these questions without making the customer work too hard.

## Start with the product, not the decoration

One common mistake is starting a packaging project by choosing colours and fonts.

Before that, the designer needs to understand the product.

For example, packaging for an organic food product may need a very different visual approach from packaging for a cosmetic product or a premium snack.

The design should reflect the product, its audience and the position the brand wants to occupy.

## The front of the pack has limited time

The front panel usually has to communicate the most important information quickly.

Depending on the product, this might include:
- Brand name
- Product name
- Main product benefit
- Key ingredient or feature
- Quantity
- Important visual element

That doesn't mean everything should be made large.

The goal is hierarchy — helping the eye understand what to read first, second and third.

## The back panel needs a different approach

The back of a package often contains more information:
- Ingredients
- Usage instructions
- Product information
- Manufacturer details
- Contact information
- Regulatory information
- Barcode and other required elements

Here, readability becomes just as important as visual appeal.

A beautiful package that makes important information difficult to read isn't doing its job well.

## Packaging should also work technically

A design can look perfect on screen and still create problems during printing.

Before finalising a packaging design, things such as the following need attention:
- Actual dieline or packaging dimensions
- Bleed and safe areas
- Print requirements
- Colour mode
- Text readability
- Barcode placement
- Required information
- Finishing or material considerations

This is why packaging design benefits from communication between the designer, client and printer.

## Don't copy what your competitor is doing

Looking at competitors is useful.

Copying them isn't.

If every product in a category uses the same colours, layout and visual style, following the same pattern may make your product disappear rather than stand out.

The better question is: What does the customer already recognise, and where can our brand be different without becoming confusing?

## What should you give your designer?

You don't need to have the complete design figured out before approaching a designer.

But having these things ready makes the process easier:
- Product name
- Brand/logo
- Product details
- Target customer
- Packaging dimensions, if available
- Required text
- Reference examples you like
- Competitor examples, if relevant
- Printer/dieline information, if available

If some of these aren't ready, discuss that before starting.

## When should you invest more in packaging?

If your product is entering retail stores, competing in a crowded category or preparing for a launch, packaging deserves more attention because it becomes part of the customer's first impression.

For a new product, it's often better to think about the packaging and brand identity together rather than treating them as completely separate pieces.

## A simple test for your packaging

Before approving a design, put the final artwork among several competing products and look at it from a distance.

Ask:
1. Can I identify the brand and product quickly?

Then look closer:
2. Can I find the information I need without searching for it?

If the answer to both is yes, the design is probably doing more than simply looking good.
